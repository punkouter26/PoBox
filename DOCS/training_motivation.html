What the fighter wants

The same twenty generations, drawn around motive rather than architecture: what the agent is paid for, what it costs to try, and why eleven generations of better payment changed nothing.

Tier 1

It was never underpaid. It was overcharged.

A fighter that tries to step and fails loses the rest of its round. A fighter that succeeds gains a fraction more score per step. For eleven generations the reward was made larger, clearer and better-measured — and none of that touched the ratio that actually decided the behaviour.

Tier 2

Three forces on every tick

Fifty times a second the fighter is scored on four things at once, multiplied rather than added. Two more forces sit outside that product: the cost of falling, and a gate that cancels the gait score entirely if the legs never swap.

Tier 3

One round, as the fighter experiences it

Three thousand steps, sixty seconds. The fighter falls roughly every eighty-five steps and is stood back up each time, losing a twenty-five-step recovery window. It never runs out of chances — that limit was removed at gen 13 because any fixed budget binds exactly when the fighter starts stepping.

The ladder it climbs

Commanded speed rises in six rungs. Each unlocks only when smoothed reward clears a gate — and the gates fall as the task gets harder, because a fighter that never steps earns less at every rung.

Three of the twenty generations produced curves that looked like success and behaviour that was not: a blind sensor, a rotated ankle, and a leg held permanently in the air. Each was found by watching the fighter, never by reading the chart.
